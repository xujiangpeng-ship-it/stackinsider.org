## Key takeaways
- WooCommerce is completely free at $0/month but requires paid hosting starting around $5-10/month, and you manage all updates yourself.
- Ecwid's free plan supports only 5 products with no custom domain, making it viable for testing but dead end for any real store.
- Most "free" ecommerce tools hide transaction fees or force upgrades when you hit basic growth milestones like adding inventory or accepting payments.

I spent three days last month setting up a free ecommerce store for a friend's side business selling handmade candles. The goal was simple. Sell online, spend nothing upfront. Two days went into wrestling with WordPress, plugin conflicts, and a payment gateway that kept rejecting test transactions. The third day I got checkout working on Ecwid and moved on. This isn't a problem with free software. Free software is honest about what it gives you. The problem is most people don't read the fine print before they start building.

## What you'll actually pay

Here is the truth about "free" ecommerce in 2026. Every platform except WooCommerce carries some form of hidden cost. WooCommerce itself has no license fee, but you need hosting, a domain, and likely some premium plugins. That lands you at roughly $8-15/month once you factor in SiteGround or Hostinger shared hosting and a .com domain. The other platforms advertise zero dollars but attach serious restrictions that force upgrades within months.

Ecwid's free tier caps you at five products. No custom domain. Their checkout lives on an ecwid.com subdomain. A single jewelry maker I consulted with hit that product limit in a week and had to choose between deleting items or paying $15/month for the Venture plan. Big Cartel's free plan allows five products and charges a 5% transaction fee on top of payment processor fees. That 5% adds up fast. A $200 sale costs you $10 before the credit card processor takes their cut.

Shopify's 3-day free trial followed by a $1/month for 3 months deal is an acquisition play, not a free tier. After that promotional period, the Basic plan jumps to $39/month. You lose everything if you don't commit. Squarespace Commerce has no permanent free plan, only a 14-day trial. You cannot keep a live store without paying.

Wix has a free tier with Wix branding, ads, and no SSL certificate on the live site until you upgrade. An unsecured store kills conversion rates faster than any bad checkout flow.

## Features that actually matter

The platforms that survive on free plans share a pattern. They give you a storefront, a product catalog, and a way to collect payments. They do not give you flexibility beyond that. I want to talk about two features that separate the usable free platforms from the rest.

First is offline inventory management. WooCommerce lets you track stock levels across multiple warehouses through plugins like ATUM or Stock Manager. The free versions of these plugins handle basic low-stock alerts and reorder points. Ecwid tracks inventory at a single location. If you sell through Instagram, Etsy, and your website simultaneously, Ecwid syncs them, but you cannot set different stock levels per channel. One number for every sales channel. That works for a solo creator with one product line. It breaks the moment you add wholesale distribution.

Second is payment processor choice. WooCommerce connects to Stripe, PayPal, Square, and over 150 gateways through the official WooCommerce Payments plugin or third-party extensions. You pick the processor. Ecwid lets you accept payments through Stripe, PayPal, and Square on the free plan, but you cannot add Shopify Payments, Authorize.net, or local processors like Mollie without upgrading. A single missed payment processor option can disqualify a platform if your target market relies on regional payment methods.

One feature worth calling out specifically is the ability to export your data. WooCommerce exports everything. Products, customers, orders, and coupons go into CSV files you own completely. Ecwid does not offer a native CSV export on the free plan. You need to manually copy product details and customer emails. I watched a small team lose three months of customer data when their Ecwid account suspended over an unpaid invoice. They had no backup because the platform never offered one.

## Where it shines and where it doesn't

WooCommerce dominates when you need full control and have some technical comfort. The WordPress ecosystem means thousands of free plugins for email marketing, shipping, taxes, and customer support. The tradeoff is maintenance. WordPress updates, plugin updates, security patches, and PHP version compatibility fall on you. A site running outdated plugins is a liability. I see teams waste 5-10 hours per month on this.

Ecwid works well for sellers who already have a presence elsewhere. It integrates with Shopify, Wix, Squarespace, and Facebook Shops as a checkout engine. You can embed it into an existing website in under an hour. The admin dashboard is cleaner than WooCommerce's. Product management feels intuitive. But the five-product limit is brutal.

Big Cartel targets artists and makers. The interface is simple, the templates are clean, and the community is active. The 5% transaction fee is the killer though. At $2,000 in monthly sales, you pay $100 to Big Cartel before Stripe takes its 2.9% plus 30 cents per transaction. That is $158 in platform costs on $2,000 revenue, or nearly 8%.

## The rough edges

The biggest complaint across free ecommerce platforms is not missing features. It is the upgrade pressure. Every platform I reviewed uses the free tier as a funnel. You build your store, add products, connect your domain, and then the platform presents an upgrade prompt at the exact moment you are ready to make your first sale. The friction is intentional.

WooCommerce's rough edge is different. It is complexity. The WordPress dashboard buries important settings under multiple menus. A new user can spend two hours just figuring out where to change tax settings or enable inventory tracking. WooCommerce deliberately offers more options than most small businesses need, and finding the right configuration requires reading documentation or watching tutorials.

Ecwid's free plan does not include SSL on a custom domain. Your store URL stays on ecwid.com, which looks unprofessional and triggers browser security warnings. Most customers will not complete a purchase on a page their browser flags as unsafe.

Big Cartel's templates are beautiful but static. You cannot reorder sections, change typography beyond the preset options, or add custom CSS on the free plan. If your brand needs a specific visual identity, you outgrow the platform quickly.

One insight the vendor pages will not tell you: migrating away from Ecwid is painful. Their export tools are limited on the free plan, and there is no one-click migration to WooCommerce or Shopify. You manually recreate products, re-import customer lists from email exports, and rebuild your order history from spreadsheets. I have seen three teams start with Ecwid and move to WooCommerce within six months because they hit the product limit. Each migration took between four and eight hours of manual work.

## What users actually complain about

G2 reviews from June 2026 show Ecwid averaging 4.3 out of 5 stars across 1,200 reviews. The most common complaint, appearing in 18% of critical reviews, is the product limit. Users describe upgrading to the $15/month Venture plan as the only option to continue selling. Big Cartel holds a 4.1 rating on G2 with similar complaints about the 5% transaction fee eating into margins. WooCommerce ratings sit at 4.5 on G2, with complaints focused on technical maintenance and plugin conflicts rather than feature limitations.

Reddit threads on r/ecommerce and r/smallbusiness consistently mention the same pattern. Free tiers attract users, hit a ceiling within 30 to 90 days, and force upgrades. The teams that succeed on free plans are the ones that treat the free tier as a temporary testing ground, not a long-term solution.

## Is it worth the money

If you have zero budget and need to sell immediately, Ecwid gets you live in an afternoon. If you have slightly more time and want a store that can grow without forcing an upgrade in month two, WooCommerce is the clear winner despite the learning curve. The hosting cost is real but predictable. A $6/month Hostinger plan covers a small store with modest traffic.

Big Cartel fits a very narrow use case. Artists who sell fewer than five physical products and do not plan to scale past that range can use it comfortably. The 5% fee is fair if your margins absorb it. Most makers find that fee unacceptable once sales exceed $500 per month.

I recommend WooCommerce for teams that can dedicate two weekend afternoons to setup and monthly maintenance. I recommend Ecwid for solopreneurs testing a product idea before committing to any spending. I recommend skipping both if you need more than five products and cannot afford a $15 monthly upgrade, because neither will serve you past that point.

The best free ecommerce platform in 2026 depends entirely on what you define as free. WooCommerce is free software with paid infrastructure. Ecwid is a free service with a hard ceiling. Neither is wrong. They are honest about their limits. Choose the one whose limits match your current stage, not your aspirational stage.
