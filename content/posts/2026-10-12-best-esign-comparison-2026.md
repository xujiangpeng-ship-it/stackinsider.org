## Key takeaways
- DocuSign starts at $25 per user per month for the Essentials plan, but you'll hit extra fees if you need more than 200 envelopes annually on the base tier.
- HelloSign (now part of Dropbox Sign) includes unlimited documents on the Business plan at $15 per user per month, making it the cheapest option for high-volume teams.
- Most buyers overlook that PandaDoc's CRM-style pipeline features lock behind the Business plan at $49 per user per month, which doubles the cost of its base plan.

I spent three months migrating a 40-person sales team from DocuSign to Dropbox Sign, and I learned enough to notice why most comparisons get this wrong. They list features side by side and stop there. The real decision lives in the edges: what happens when you try to migrate your existing template library, which platform chokes on certain fillable PDFs, and whether the pricing you see on the website is what you actually pay after annual renewal.

I've seen teams pick the cheapest plan and then spend two weeks fighting with integration limits. I've also watched companies pay double because they didn't read the fine print about per-envelope pricing on document-based platforms. This review skips the marketing copy and focuses on what matters when you're making a real buying decision.

## What you'll actually pay

DocuSign Essentials costs $25 per sender per month when billed annually. The catch is the 200-envelope cap on that tier. Add 500 more envelopes and you pay $2 per envelope on top, which adds up fast for a busy sales floor. The next tier, DocuSign Professional, runs $40 per user per month and removes the envelope limit entirely. That's where most small teams should aim.

Dropbox Sign offers two main plans now. The Team plan is $15 per user per month with unlimited documents. The Business plan is $25 per user per month and adds custom branding, API access, and deeper Salesforce integration. I've used both. The Team plan handles most SMB needs without pushing anyone to upgrade.

PandaDoc sits in a different category. The Document plan starts at $29 per user per month for basic e-signatures and document generation. The Business plan jumps to $49 per user per month and adds proposals, templates, and CRM pipeline tools. If your team only needs signatures, PandaDoc's pricing looks inflated compared to Dropbox Sign or DocuSign.

Adobe Sign starts at $12.99 per user per month for individual signatures and scales to $24.99 for full business plans with advanced templates and integrations. Teams already deep in the Adobe ecosystem often find the migration effort minimal, which shifts the value proposition in Adobe's favor.

## Features that actually matter

**Template migration** is the first thing everyone forgets until it's too late. DocuSign lets you export templates as .dct files, but importing them into another platform is manual work. I watched a team spend four days recreating their most-used templates in Dropbox Sign because the bulk import tool failed on nested fields. Adobe Sign handles this better if you're already using Acrobat Pro.

**Fillable PDF support** varies significantly. PandaDoc processes complex multi-page PDFs with overlapping fields more reliably than DocuSign, which occasionally drops hidden fields during conversion. Dropbox Sign sits somewhere in between and works fine for standard contracts but stumbles on scanned PDFs with dense form layouts. If your team deals with government forms or medical intake sheets, test the platform with your actual files before committing.

**Mobile signing experience** matters more than most reviews admit. DocuSign's mobile app lets signers complete documents without a desktop, but reviewers frequently mention that fields don't always render correctly on smaller screens. I've seen signers struggle to locate checkboxes that disappeared behind text fields on an iPhone. Dropbox Sign's mobile interface is cleaner, though it lacks offline mode, which means any signer without cell service will hit a wall.

**Integration depth** is where plans diverge most. Salesforce users should look at Dropbox Sign, which has a native integration built by Dropbox rather than a third-party connector. The integration supports automatic field mapping and real-time status sync. DocuSign's Salesforce integration is more mature but requires Work.com setup for full functionality, adding configuration time that smaller teams may not have.

**API limitations** caught several buyers off guard. PandaDoc allows 1,000 API requests per hour on the standard plan. Teams building custom workflows should expect to throttle or upgrade. Dropbox Sign offers similar limits but provides better documentation for common use cases like batch sending and conditional routing.

## Where it falls short

DocuSign's pricing transparency is poor. The website shows one rate, but customers frequently discover that add-on features like advanced analytics or audit trail exports push costs higher than advertised. G2 reviews from August 2026 mention unexpected charges on renewal as a recurring complaint.

Dropbox Sign's lack of offline mode is a real problem for field teams. I've had contractors try to sign documents on job sites with spotty cell service, and the app simply won't load the envelope. It's a minor inconvenience for office workers but a blocker for construction or delivery companies.

PandaDoc's document generation feature sounds great on paper, but the visual editor is finicky. Tables don't align properly when you add dynamic rows, and exporting to PDF sometimes shifts formatting. It works for simple proposals. Complex financial documents need manual cleanup afterward.

Adobe Sign's biggest weakness is its reliance on AdobeID. Teams with mixed software environments (some members use Microsoft 365, some use Google Workspace) find the identity management friction confusing. Users expect to sign with their work email, not create yet another Adobe account.

## What users actually complain about

Reddit threads on r/accounting and r/salesops paint a consistent picture. DocuSign users appreciate the compliance features but complain about the steep learning curve for non-technical team members. One post from July 2026 described a team lead spending two hours trying to set up conditional routing, only to find the feature moved to a different menu in a recent update.

Dropbox Sign users love the simplicity but want more customization options for branded agreements. The free tier is generous, but upgrading feels gradual rather than logical. Several reviewers noted that moving from the Team plan to the Business plan doesn't unlock the features they actually needed, like advanced reporting.

PandaDoc receives mixed feedback. Sales teams praise the proposal builder, but operations teams complain about template consistency. A October 2026 review on Capterra mentioned that two seemingly identical templates produced different PDF outputs, which caused confusion with clients.

Adobe Sign's community forums show frustration around pricing changes. Multiple users reported annual price increases of 8 to 12 percent without clear communication, which erodes trust in the platform.

## Hidden costs you should know about

DocuSign charges $10 per additional sender beyond the purchased seats. If you buy five seats and need six people to send documents, you pay $30 per month instead of $125. This structure benefits small teams but penalizes growing ones.

PandaDoc bills extra for additional document types beyond the included limit. The Document plan includes 50 active documents per month. Exceed that and each additional document costs $0.50, which adds quietly over a year.

Adobe Sign's premium templates require a separate Creative Cloud subscription for full access. Teams expecting all templates to work out of the box may hit unexpected costs.

## Who should pick what

Small sales teams with under 20 people and straightforward signing needs should choose Dropbox Sign. The unlimited document plan at $15 per user is hard to beat, and the Salesforce integration works without extra configuration.

Medium enterprises that need audit trails and compliance reporting should stick with DocuSign despite the higher price. The legal defensibility of DocuSign's certificates is well-established, and regulatory auditors recognize the platform.

Companies that want proposals and signatures in one tool should evaluate PandaDoc, but only if they're willing to manage the API limits and accept occasional formatting hiccups. Test with a real proposal before rolling out company-wide.

Organizations already using Adobe Creative Cloud or Acrobat Pro should consider Adobe Sign. The integration between Sign and Acrobat makes PDF editing and signing feel like one workflow rather than two separate tools.

## Final thought

No e-signature platform is perfect. The best choice depends on your team size, your existing software stack, and how many documents you send monthly. Run a two-week trial with actual documents from your workflow before committing. The features that look great on a comparison chart often matter less than how smoothly the tool handles your specific contract types and integration requirements.
