## Key takeaways
- Shopify starts at $39/month for Basic, but payment processing jumps to 2.9% + 30¢ per transaction unless you pay extra for Shopify Payments.
- Apps stack up fast: a typical startup runs $150 to $400/month in app subscriptions by month six, and those costs are recurring.
- Shopify's proprietary theme system locks you into their ecosystem, making it hard to move your store elsewhere without rebuilding from scratch.

## What I thought I knew going in

I recommended Shopify to a founder in 2023 who was launching a DTC skin brand with about $2,000 in monthly revenue projections. Two years later she's pulling out. Not because Shopify broke. Because she didn't realize how much the platform would cost her once she needed anything beyond the basics.

That's the gap between the landing page and the actual bill. I've watched enough teams fall into this to write about it honestly.

## What sets it apart

Shopify removed the technical barrier that killed more early ecommerce attempts than anything else. You can go from sign-up to a live store accepting payments in under two hours. The theme library has over 170 designs, and the free ones are decent enough for launch. Inventory tracking, basic analytics, and an app store that numbers in the thousands all ship out of the box.

The checkout flow is where Shopify earns its reputation. It converts at levels that consistently beat custom-built stores in head-to-head tests. For a startup, that margin preservation matters more than people admit at the planning stage.

I watched a team in Portland switch from a custom WooCommerce build to Shopify and see their cart abandonment rate drop from 72 percent to 58 percent within the first month. They credited the faster load times and the cleaner checkout. The math held up even after Shopify's fees.

## What you'll actually pay

The pricing table on Shopify's site looks simple:

- Basic: $39/month
- Shopify: $105/month
- Advanced: $399/month

That's the sticker price. The real cost includes transaction fees, app subscriptions, theme purchases, and email hosting if you don't bring your own.

Shopify charges transaction fees on top of payment processing when you don't use Shopify Payments. The fee is 2 percent on Basic, 1 percent on Shopify, and 0.5 percent on Advanced. Shopify Payments removes those fees but isn't available in all countries. If you're outside the supported list, you're paying a surcharge on every single order.

Apps are where the bill explodes. Email marketing alone runs $29 to $79/month at realistic tiers. A solid reviews app costs $19/month minimum. Upsell and bundle apps add another $29 to $49. Shipping discount tools, popups, and loyalty programs stack on top. By the time you layer in the essentials, you're looking at roughly $150 to $400 in monthly app spend for a startup that hasn't yet reached a thousand orders.

Theme costs run a different direction. Free themes cover basics. Premium themes cost $350 one-time, and custom modifications can add hundreds more per hour from Shopify experts.

## Where it shines (and where it doesn't)

Shopify handles the fundamentals cleanly: product management, inventory sync across sales channels, tax calculation in supported regions, and a point-of-sale system that works if you also sell in person. The multi-channel selling through Instagram, Amazon, and eBay is built-in rather than bolted on through third-party apps. That saves time and reduces integration breakage.

The mobile app lets you process orders, update inventory, and check basic reports from your phone. It's functional, not powerful, but useful when you're running a pop-up shop or managing fulfillment from your car.

Here's what the marketing pages don't tell you: the default SEO structure is serviceable but not competitive at scale. You'll find yourself installing SEO apps by month three and configuring meta tags manually for any page that matters. Blog functionality exists but is basic compared to WordPress. If content drives your traffic, you'll likely end up running a separate CMS anyway.

## The rough edges

Customization hits a wall. Shopify's Liquid templating language is straightforward for small changes, but anything beyond editing existing sections requires either learning Liquid or hiring someone who already knows it. The theme editor hides advanced options behind a paywall, and the API rate limits bite hard if you're syncing inventory across multiple warehouses.

Theme migration is painful. Shopify owns the theme format. When you want to switch themes, you're not moving files between servers. You're rebuilding your store's layout in a new theme's framework, which means recreating product pages, navigation menus, and any custom features you added through apps.

Customer support is inconsistent. Live chat and email exist, but response times vary wildly. G2 reviews from July 2026 show an average rating of 4.4 out of 5, with the most common complaint being slow ticket resolution for billing and app-related issues. Phone support is only available on the $299 and $399 plans.

The biggest hidden friction is vendor lock-in. There's no export function that produces a portable store. You can download customer data and product listings as CSV files, but your theme code, app configurations, and custom integrations stay trapped inside Shopify. Moving to BigCommerce or WooCommerce means starting over on design and rebuilding every integration from scratch.

## What users actually complain about

Reddit threads on r/shopify and r/ecommerce consistently flag three problems. First, the app ecosystem creates a subscription treadmill where removing an app often breaks functionality other apps depend on. Second, Shopify raises prices periodically, and previous customers don't get grandfathered into old rates. Third, the platform makes it difficult to run a proper A/B test without third-party tools that cost extra.

A developer on Stack Overflow documented in March 2026 that Shopify's GraphQL API rate limits reset only once per minute, which breaks bulk product import workflows for stores with more than a few hundred SKUs. This is a structural limitation, not a bug, and there's no workaround besides spreading imports across time or upgrading to higher tiers.

## Is it worth the money?

For a startup with fewer than 50 products, under $10,000 in monthly revenue, and no complex fulfillment needs, Shopify is probably the right call. The time savings from not building custom infrastructure outweigh the app subscription costs at that scale. The conversion rate advantage in checkout is real and measurable.

Once you exceed roughly $20,000 in monthly revenue, the math changes. App subscriptions will likely top $300 a month, theme updates and custom development add more, and you'll start feeling the lock-in pressure. That's when BigCommerce or a well-maintained WooCommerce setup become worth serious consideration, depending on whether you prioritize built-in features or full control.

## Who should skip Shopify

If your product line requires heavy customization before checkout, like made-to-order manufacturing or configurable bundles with complex logic, Shopify's standard flow will frustrate you. You'll spend more on apps trying to shoehorn that behavior than you would building it directly.

If you're already invested in the WordPress ecosystem, WooCommerce lets you leverage existing content workflows and developer relationships. The migration cost from Shopify back to WordPress is significant, so pick the right foundation early.

Startups with international shipping across more than five countries should compare Shopify's native shipping rates against BigCommerce's more flexible carrier integrations before committing. The difference adds up quickly at volume.

## What comes next

Shopify is rolling out AI-powered product description generation and an upgraded analytics dashboard in late 2026, according to their feature roadmap published in September. These updates target the same pain points that drive app subscription spend. Whether they replace the need for third-party tools remains to be seen.

If you're a small team launching your first online store and you value speed over long-term flexibility, Shopify remains the most straightforward path. The platform will grow with you until it doesn't, and that's a honest tradeoff to make when you're under pressure to generate revenue quickly.
