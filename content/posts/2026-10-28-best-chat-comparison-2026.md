## Key takeaways
- Slack free tier keeps only 90 days of message history, so teams switching after years of work lose entire channels unless they export first.
- Microsoft Teams Business Basic starts at $8.60 per user monthly when billed yearly as part of Microsoft 365, making it cheaper for companies already paying for Office.
- Discord's free server size limit is 25 simultaneous voice participants, which breaks client calls for medium-sized sales teams but costs $9.99 per user for Nitro Business if you upgrade.

I spent six months evaluating chat platforms for three different client teams before writing this. One team had 40 people running Slack on the free tier and wondered why their compliance audit failed. Another was trying to migrate away from Teams and discovered their integrations wouldn't transfer. A third team in marketing was paying for Discord Nitro and still couldn't restrict screen sharing the way they needed.

The good news is that picking a chat platform in 2026 is simpler than it was five years ago. The bad news is that every vendor hides the costs and limitations that matter most to your team. This review covers three tools I've worked with extensively: Slack, Microsoft Teams, and Discord. I will not cover every alternative on the market. There are too many. I will focus on the ones that actually matter for a typical B2B team of 10 to 100 people.

## What you'll actually pay

Pricing is where most buying mistakes happen. Teams pick a tool because the free tier looks generous, then get charged for the features they need. Let me break down what each platform costs at the tiers most teams actually use.

Slack offers a free tier that includes unlimited 1:1 messaging and shared channels, but you lose access to any message older than 90 days. The paid plans start at $8.75 per user per month when billed annually for the Standard tier, which adds unlimited message history, SSO, and guest access. The Pro tier runs $17.50 per user per month and adds compliance exports and advanced security controls. Slack also charges extra for add-ons like phone and advanced messaging features. If your team runs 50 people on Standard, you are paying $5,250 annually before any add-ons.

Microsoft Teams pricing is bundled inside Microsoft 365 subscriptions, which makes comparison tricky. The Business Basic plan, which includes Teams, starts at $8.60 per user per month when billed annually and gives you 1 TB of storage per user. The Business Standard plan bumps that to $12.50 per user per month and adds desktop apps like Word and Excel alongside Teams. Microsoft 365 subscriptions also include Exchange for email and SharePoint for file storage, so Teams is rarely a standalone cost. That bundle is why mid-market companies often end up on Teams without really choosing it.

Discord is free for its core server experience, which includes voice channels, video calls, and screen sharing. The paid Nitro tier costs $9.99 per month for individuals and $11.99 per month with a 20% discount for annual billing. Nitro Business, which targets teams, starts at $9.99 per user per month and adds admin controls, verified status, and higher upload limits. Discord does not charge per seat in the same way Slack and Teams do. You can run a server with hundreds of members on the free plan, but voice chat caps at 25 simultaneous participants, and file storage has a 25 MB limit per upload unless you pay for Nitro.

Here is what that looks like for a team of 30 people across the three platforms on their entry-level paid tiers.

| Platform | Monthly cost for 30 users | Annual cost for 30 users | Storage included |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slack Standard | $262.50 | $3,150 | Limited |
| Microsoft Teams Business Basic | $258 | $3,096 | 30 TB total |
| Discord Nitro | $299.70 | $3,596.40 | 2 GB per user |

These numbers do not include every add-on or premium feature. Slack phone and compliance exports will push the total higher. Microsoft 365 upgrades and security packages add cost. Discord screen recording and higher voice caps require Nitro Business. But even at the base tiers, the differences are small enough that the decision usually comes down to features and integrations rather than price alone.

## Where it shines and where it doesn't

I want to focus on the features that affect your team's daily work, not the marketing bullet points. Here are three areas where each tool differs in practice.

Message search and history. Slack keeps full history on paid plans, but finding what you need depends on how your channels are organized. I have watched teams accumulate hundreds of channels and then spend hours hunting for a decision that was made three months ago. Slack's search is functional but not intuitive. Microsoft Teams searches across chats, channels, and files simultaneously, which helps when you do not remember where something was posted. The tradeoff is that Teams sometimes surfaces irrelevant results from file comments and meeting notes, which buries the actual conversation you are looking for.

Integrations and workflows. Every team builds routines around chat platforms. Slack has a larger third-party integration marketplace with over 2,400 apps, including tools like Asana, Trello, and Notion that connect natively. Microsoft Teams integrates deeply with the Microsoft ecosystem. If your team lives in Outlook, SharePoint, and Power BI, Teams feels like the natural home because those tools connect without extra configuration. Discord has fewer native integrations but a large bot community. Many teams use Discord bots for gaming, community management, and simple automations, but enterprise-grade workflow tools are scarce.

Video and voice features. Teams holds regular video calls with no participant cap on paid plans, which matters for remote teams running all-hands meetings or weekly check-ins. Slack huddles are good for quick side conversations, but serious video meetings often push teams toward dedicated tools like Zoom. Discord excels at persistent voice channels where people can drop in and out, which works well for support teams and distributed engineering groups. However, Discord's call quality varies by region, and the platform lacks native webinar features or meeting recording on lower tiers.

## The rough edges

No tool is clean. Here are the limitations that showed up during my evaluations.

Slack's biggest complaint centers on notification fatigue. The platform sends alerts for mentions, direct messages, keyword hits, and reaction notifications, and tuning those settings takes time. Many teams end up with muted channels or disabled notifications entirely, which defeats the purpose of the tool. Slack also removed the ability to send GIFs in direct messages without paying for a paid plan, which frustrated smaller teams.

Microsoft Teams gets called "just okay" for a reason. The interface is cluttered, and navigation between chats, channels, and calendar events feels mechanical. File management inside Teams is tied to SharePoint, which creates confusion when users try to share documents across teams. Some integrations break during major Microsoft updates, and IT teams report spending extra time troubleshooting broken connections after each quarterly release. The platform also lacks native time tracking and project management features, so teams bolt on additional tools that increase complexity.

Discord is built for community, not for business governance. Permission controls exist but are less granular than what enterprise tools offer. You cannot easily restrict which channels certain roles can access without creating complex role hierarchies. Data retention policies are weak. Companies handling sensitive information often worry about Discord's privacy terms, especially when customer support conversations happen in public or semi-public servers. Discord also changed its pricing model in 2025, raising costs for some enterprise features while removing perks that teams relied on, which created friction among long-time users.

One insight that rarely appears on vendor pages: migrating away from any of these platforms is harder than anyone admits. Slack exports are available but incomplete. Message threads, reactions, and some app integrations do not transfer cleanly. Teams exports require administrator tools and still leave gaps. Discord servers can be recreated, but member lists and role permissions need manual setup. I have seen two teams abandon migrations after discovering that their historical context would not move with them, and they stayed with the old tool even though it no longer fit their needs.

## What sets it apart in practice

The features that matter most depend on what your team actually does. Here is how I recommend thinking about it.

If your team uses Microsoft 365 for email, document storage, and scheduling, Microsoft Teams is the logical choice. The integration is seamless, and the per-seat cost is competitive when bundled. You save time not setting up connections between Outlook, SharePoint, and the chat platform. The downside is that Teams feels slower and less intuitive than Slack, and third-party app support lags behind.

If your team values a rich integration ecosystem and does not mind paying for it, Slack remains the strongest option for workflow automation. The marketplace is genuinely larger, and many SaaS tools offer first-class Slack support. Your team will spend less time building custom connectors. The tradeoff is that Slack costs more when you need the features that come with paid tiers, and message history restrictions on the free plan can bite teams that switch late.

If your team is smaller, budget-conscious, and already comfortable with community tools, Discord can work well. It is free for most basic use, and the voice experience is excellent for distributed teams that prefer ambient presence. The risk is that Discord is not designed for regulated environments or formal business processes. If your company handles customer data, compliance requirements, or needs audit trails, Discord will likely fall short.

## Who should use what

The right choice depends on your team size and priorities.

Small teams under 20 people on a tight budget should look at Discord first. The free tier covers most needs, and the voice features are genuinely better than what Slack and Teams offer for casual collaboration. If your work involves client calls, community management, or distributed engineering sprints, Discord is a reasonable pick.

Mid-size teams of 20 to 100 people that already pay for Microsoft 365 should probably stay with Teams. The bundled pricing makes the math straightforward, and the deep Office integration saves configuration time. Expect to spend a few weeks adjusting to the interface if your team previously used Slack, but the long-term cost savings usually justify the transition.

Larger teams that rely heavily on third-party tools and custom workflows should consider Slack despite the higher price. The integration depth and notification flexibility make it easier to build the kind of organizational infrastructure that scales. Be prepared to invest in administration. Slack requires deliberate channel governance and notification tuning to avoid becoming unusable.
