## Key takeaways
- Shopify's basic plan costs $39/month plus a 2.9% transaction fee, but construction-specific apps like bulk pricing and quote requests add another $50 to $150 monthly on top.
- WooCommerce looks free but real hosting, security, and essential plugin costs typically run $75 to $200 per month once you add what construction needs.
- BigCommerce charges $299/month for the Plus plan with built-in B2B features, saving you roughly $100 monthly compared to bolt-on solutions on other platforms.

I spent three years helping general contractors, subcontractors, and building supply distributors pick their ecommerce stack. The construction industry has unique requirements that most platforms weren't built for: large item bundles, project-based pricing, trade-only accounts, and orders that span multiple delivery dates. I've watched teams waste thousands on platforms that looked great in demos but fell apart when a foreman tried to reorder 47 boxes of drywall at 6 AM on a job site.

## What you'll actually pay

Shopify is the easiest platform to start with and the most expensive to customize for construction. The Basic plan runs $39/month. You get the storefront, basic reporting, and 24/7 support. But nearly everything a construction company needs sits behind paid apps. Bulk pricing starts at $9.99/month. A quote-to-order app runs $19.99/month. Trade customer verification costs another $14.99/month. Those three apps alone nearly double your monthly bill.

WooCommerce costs nothing to install on WordPress. That's the trap. You pay for hosting, a management plugin, SSL, backups, and then the extensions. SiteGround's GrowBig plan for a medium-volume store runs about $14.95/month per site. Cloudways starts at $11/month. An e-commerce extension pack with bulk discounts, trade pricing, and delivery scheduling will set you back $300 to $600 per year in one-time fees or recurring subscriptions. Your total comes to roughly $100 to $200 monthly when you include developer hours for initial setup and ongoing maintenance.

BigCommerce Plus runs $299/month. This is where the value shows for construction. You get volume pricing rules, customer groups with tiered pricing, quote management, and a B2B suite built in. No app subscriptions required for the features other platforms charge separately. You still pay transaction fees unless you use BigCommerce Payments, which ranges from 2.9% plus 30 cents to 2.5% plus 30 cents depending on your plan.

Square Online starts free but takes 2.9% plus 30 cents per transaction. The paid Commerce plan is $29/month and adds inventory sync and abandoned cart recovery. It lacks the B2B depth construction teams need, though simple product catalogs work fine.

Magento Open Source is technically free but realistically costs $5,000 to $15,000 in development to reach a functional state for construction. Adobe Commerce starts at $22,000 annually for the license alone. Only consider this if you have 50 or more SKUs and an IT team handling deployment.

## Features that actually matter for construction

Bulk reorder functionality separates construction ecommerce from regular retail. A roofing crew doesn't browse products one by one. They place the same order every Tuesday: 60 bundles of shingles, 12 rolls of felt, 200 nails. The best construction stores let you save repeat orders, set up subscription deliveries, and reorder from a single click. Shopify achieves this through the Order Hero app and custom cart rules. WooCommerce uses the Repurchase plugin and custom checkout flows. BigCommerce handles this natively with its B2B bulk pricing tables.

Trade pricing and net terms matter if you sell to other contractors. Many construction purchases happen on 30-day terms rather than credit cards. BigCommerce supports net terms natively on Plus and Enterprise plans. Shopify requires third-party payment gateways and apps to handle invoice-based payments, which adds friction and cost.

Project-based ordering is rare but useful. A contractor building a custom home might want to group orders by phase: foundation, framing, rough-in, finish. BigCommerce lets you tag orders with project codes. WooCommerce requires custom development for this level of grouping. Shopify stores typically rely on line-item notes, which don't provide real filtering or reporting capability.

Mobile experience is non-negotiable. Foremen and procurement managers place orders from job sites, not offices. Shopify's mobile checkout converts well. WooCommerce stores often feel slow on 3G connections because the admin-heavy WordPress backend bleeds into the frontend performance. BigCommerce's mobile experience sits somewhere in between, solid but not as polished as Shopify.

Delivery scheduling matters for heavy or bulky materials. You can't ship a pallet of brick through a standard courier. BigCommerce integrates with freight carriers and allows delivery date windows. Shopify offers this through shipping apps that start at $19.99/month. WooCommerce requires custom development or premium plugins costing $100 to $300 per year.

## Where it falls short

Shopify's biggest limitation for construction is the app dependency loop. Every feature beyond the base platform adds another monthly charge and another integration to maintain. When an app updates its API, your checkout flow might break. I watched a lumber supplier lose two weeks of order processing because a bulk pricing app update conflicted with their custom theme.

WooCommerce's maintenance burden is the real cost nobody mentions. WordPress core updates, plugin conflicts, security patches, and backup failures require ongoing attention. If your team doesn't have someone comfortable with technical troubleshooting, you'll pay a developer on retainer or deal with downtime during critical sales periods. One flooring distributor I worked with spent $4,200 in a single year on maintenance and emergency fixes after migrating from Shopify.

BigCommerce lacks the depth of the Shopify app marketplace. If you need a very specific construction feature, you might not find a native BigCommerce app for it. The built-in B2B tools cover most needs, but niche requirements fall through the cracks. The platform also has a smaller community, which means fewer pre-built themes designed for industrial or construction aesthetics.

Square Online simply doesn't go far enough for construction. The product catalog is too basic, there are no B2B features, and bulk discounting is limited. It works for a small handyman selling supplies but fails for any operation doing more than $50,000 in annual online sales.

## A detail most reviews miss

Shopify's shipping rate calculator doesn't handle freight well without significant customization. Construction products weigh thousands of pounds. The default shipping method charges by weight and dimension, which works for boxed items but breaks when you're shipping lumber bundles, pallets of tile, or containers of fixtures. You'll need a shipping app like Shiphero or Freight Rules, and even then, rate shopping between freight carriers requires manual workarounds. I've seen construction stores undercharge for freight on 15% of their orders because the platform calculated shipping as parcel instead of LTL. BigCommerce handles freight rates more gracefully through its native integrations with carriers like UPS Freight and FedEx Freight.

## My recommendation

If you're a construction supply company doing under $500,000 in annual online sales with fewer than 300 SKUs, start with Shopify. The app ecosystem will handle your needs, and the migration path upward is smooth. Budget an extra $80 to $150 monthly for construction-specific apps.

If you're doing $500,000 to $5 million annually and selling to trade customers, BigCommerce Plus at $299/month is the better investment. You skip the app fees, the B2B features are native, and freight integration is cleaner from day one. The higher upfront cost pays for itself within three months when you factor in app subscriptions and reduced troubleshooting time.

If you have an internal technical team and unlimited customization needs, WooCommerce gives you the most control. Just budget for ongoing maintenance work, not just initial setup. The total cost of ownership over two years usually exceeds BigCommerce by a meaningful margin.

Avoid Square Online for anything beyond the smallest operations. It's convenient but insufficient for the complexity construction ecommerce demands.
