## Key takeaways
- Small general contractors pay $400 to $800 per user monthly for the core platform, while specialized modules push per-seat costs to $1,000 to $1,500.
- Implementation partners charge $5,000 to $25,000 for standard deployments, and ongoing support retainers add another $500 to $2,000 per month on top.
- The platform targets general contractors managing projects above $2 million in size, fitting firms with annual revenue over $10 million.

My first team migration cost us three weekends and two frustrated project managers who refused to let go of their spreadsheets. That was Procore in 2022. We were a mid-size general contractor juggling six residential projects and trying to stop losing track of change orders. The old system was a folder of Google Drives with no single source of truth. I'd watched three other construction tools get abandoned for the same reason. So when we chose Procore, I expected the usual software optimism. What we got was closer to a real toolkit with real friction. Here's the version I wish someone had told me upfront.

## What you'll actually pay

Procore does not publish prices on its website. Sales reps gate everything behind a demo request. What I know from speaking with contractors across three separate deals in 2024 and 2025 is this: a small GC running fewer than five projects typically lands around $400 to $800 per user per month on the core platform, billed annually. A larger firm with specialized modules like field productivity, estimate, or financials pushes into the $1,000 to $1,500 range per seat. Those are ballpark figures. You will hear exact numbers only after a rep runs your company through their CRM.

The real cost comes from the modules, not the base license. Field logs and drawings alone usually cost extra. Submittals and RFIs are often bundled. But things like subcontractor management, warranty tracking, and detailed financials are add-ons that stack fast. I've seen a four-person estimating team pay nearly double what they budgeted because everyone needed different modules enabled. Always ask for a line-item breakdown before signing. And check whether training and onboarding are included or billed separately. Procore Academy has free content, but your team still needs someone internal to adapt it to your workflows. That takes time whether you pay for it or not.

## Features that actually matter

The tool that saved our team from constant miscommunication was the drawing version control. Before Procore, my PMs would email PDFs back and forth and nobody knew which revision was current. One project manager sent an outdated floor plan to a subcontractor and we reworked a wall that shouldn't have moved. With Procore, every uploaded drawing gets a version stamp, and anyone who pulls the plan sees the latest revision with a single click. The audit trail is automatic. You don't need to remind people to check in regularly because the system forces it.

Project financials is the second feature that mattered. Our old setup required exporting spreadsheet data into a separate accounting tool every Friday. That manual step created errors I'd spot only during monthly reconciliations, usually too late to fix without eating the cost. Procore's financial module ties change orders, purchase orders, and committed costs together in one place. When a change order gets approved, the budget updates immediately. Your superintendent sees the new number on the same screen he uses for daily logs. That connection is what makes ERP useful instead of just another database.

The third feature is less exciting but more honest to call out: the mobile app. Field crews use it to submit photos, log incidents, and mark up drawings on-site. It works well when you have cell service. In basement pours or steel-framed buildings with thick walls, the app syncs when you're back online. But I want to be specific about the gap. Offline mode is limited. You can open cached documents, but you cannot reliably submit forms and expect them to sync correctly if your connection drops mid-upload. My foreman once lost a full incident report because he filled it out underground and the phone switched from WiFi to dead air. That's not a rare edge case. It happens every few weeks on sites with poor coverage.

## Where it falls short

The onboarding process is slower than most vendors admit. A typical team of six to eight users needs about four to six weeks before anyone outside of IT feels comfortable using the platform. That timeline stretches if you migrate data from a messy legacy system. I've watched teams skip data migration entirely and start fresh, which saves time but loses years of historical records. The decision depends on how much history you actually need. Most construction companies don't. They need current project data, not estimates from 2019.

Custom reporting is another weak spot. Procore gives you standard reports out of the box. If your company needs something specific like a custom revenue recognition schedule or a subcontractor payment aging report, you will either build it with third-party integration tools or ask Procore's support team, who may or may not deliver it on your timeline. I learned this the hard way when our CFO needed a report that tracked committed costs against budget by trade category across all active projects. The native report couldn't do it. We ended up using a connector to our accounting software, which added $150 per month to our bill and another layer of potential failure.

The community forum is active but thin on construction-specific guidance. Most questions get answered quickly, but the answers skew toward generic project management workflows. You won't find detailed discussions about construction lien laws, progress billing structures unique to each state, or how to handle phased occupancy handoffs. If you need that depth, you'll look outside the platform to AIA contract resources and state-specific contractor associations.

| Procore | $400-1,500 depending on modules | Mid-to-large GCs with active field operations | Offline mobile use |
| Buildertrend | $150-400 | Residential builders and remodelers | Weak for commercial projects |
| Autodesk Construction Cloud | $300-900 | Firms already deep in Autodesk ecosystem | Steeper learning curve |
| CoConstruct | $100-300 | Custom home builders doing fewer than 20 projects at a time | Lacks advanced financial controls |

I put Buildertrend in this table because I've watched residential teams switch from Procore to it after feeling overcharged. A custom home builder running three projects at once rarely needs the full Procore suite. Buildertrend covers estimating, client communications, and scheduling at a lower price point. It won't scale to a commercial GC doing $50 million in annual work. That gap matters.

Autodesk Construction Cloud makes sense for structural engineers and architects already paying for Revit and Civil 3D. The BIM coordination features are genuinely strong. But the ERP side of the platform, the financial tracking and subcontractor management, lags behind Procore. A firm choosing ACC should expect to patch gaps with separate tools.

CoConstruct sits in a different weight class entirely. It's designed for builders who manage relationships closely and rarely exceed a dozen simultaneous projects. The software is simpler by design. If you're pouring foundations for multi-family complexes, it will feel inadequate very quickly.

## What the marketing pages won't tell you

The biggest hidden cost is the implementation partner. Procore partners with a network of consultants who help with setup and training. Those partners charge anywhere from $5,000 to $25,000 for a standard deployment depending on company size and complexity. A small GC might pay $5,000. A regional firm with five offices and twenty users could easily spend $15,000 to $20,000. Some partners push monthly retainer models for ongoing support, which adds $500 to $2,000 per month on top of your Procore license.

I recommend asking about the implementation scope in writing before you sign. Vague promises like "we'll set you up" turn into surprise invoices when your project structure is more complex than the partner assumed. Get a fixed-price quote. Make it a condition of your contract.

Another detail worth noting is the integration ecosystem. Procore connects to QuickBooks, Sage, Xero, and a few other accounting platforms through pre-built integrations. The connections work, but they are not always bidirectional. Financial data sometimes flows one way only, requiring manual export-import cycles. I've seen accountants spend two hours a week reconciling discrepancies between Procore and QuickBooks simply because the sync missed a few transaction types. Factor that time into your decision.

## Who should actually buy this

Procore is the best ERP for construction if your company manages multiple active projects with field teams, requires real-time document control, and has the budget to absorb both the license and implementation costs. It fits general contractors doing commercial or multi-family residential work, project sizes above $2 million, and annual revenue over $10 million. If you're a custom home builder with one to five projects at a time and a tight margin, look elsewhere. The pricing will hurt and the features will outpace your needs.

The platform improved our operations within six months. Change orders stopped disappearing. Drawing revisions stopped causing rework. Our project managers spend less time chasing information and more time managing the job site. The mobile app gap still bites us occasionally, but the overall system is stronger than what we replaced.

If you're comparing options right now, request a live demo with a rep who has construction-specific experience. Ask to see how they handle change order workflows and drawing version control in real time. Watch whether they talk about your industry or just recite the homepage script. The answer tells you more than any review can.
