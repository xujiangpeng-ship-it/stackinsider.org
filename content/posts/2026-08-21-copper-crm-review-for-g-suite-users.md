Copper CRM markets itself as the CRM built for Google Workspace. If you live in Gmail, Drive, and Calendar, the pitch makes sense: no more switching tabs to log a call or update a deal. But after setting it up for three different teams—two agencies and a SaaS startup—I can tell you the integration isn’t as smooth as the ads suggest.

## What you get with Copper and G Suite

Copper installs as a sidebar inside Gmail. You can create contacts, deals, and tasks without leaving your inbox. The sidebar shows recent activity, emails, and files tied to a record. Clicking a contact pulls up their LinkedIn profile, recent emails, and any open deals—useful if you’re about to hop on a call.

The Google Drive integration lets you attach files to records directly from Drive. No more digging through folders. Calendar syncs meetings to Copper, so you can log notes against the right contact afterward. There’s also a Chrome extension that adds Copper actions to LinkedIn, so you can save a prospect while scrolling their profile.

For teams already using Google Workspace, these features save time. But they’re not magic.

## Where the G Suite integration stumbles

The sidebar loads slowly. On a 2023 MacBook Air with a fast connection, it took 3-5 seconds to populate after opening an email. That adds up when you’re processing 50+ emails a day. The Chrome extension also crashes occasionally, especially if you have multiple tabs open.

Drive attachments work, but Copper doesn’t index the content. If you attach a contract PDF, you can’t search for terms inside it—only the filename. This is a dealbreaker for teams that rely on document search.

Calendar sync has quirks. Copper creates a separate calendar for deals, which clutters your Google Calendar view. You can hide it, but then you lose visibility into deal stages unless you open Copper. Meetings logged in Copper don’t automatically appear in your personal calendar unless you manually add them.

The mobile app is barebones. You can view contacts and deals, but editing is clunky. Offline mode doesn’t exist, so if you’re on a plane or in a dead zone, you’re locked out. For teams that need to update records on the go, this is a real limitation.

## What you’ll actually pay

Copper’s pricing is straightforward but not cheap. Here’s how it breaks down as of August 2026:

| Plan          | Price per user/month | Max contacts | Custom fields | Workflow automation | API access |
|---------------|----------------------|--------------|---------------|---------------------|------------|
| Basic         | $29                  | 10,000       | 10            | No                  | No         |
| Professional  | $69                  | 50,000       | 50            | Yes                 | Yes        |
| Business      | $134                 | 100,000      | 100           | Yes                 | Yes        |

The Basic plan is missing workflow automation, which is table stakes for most CRMs today. You’ll need Professional if you want to auto-assign leads or send follow-up emails based on deal stage. Business adds priority support and a dedicated account manager, but the jump from Professional is steep.

There’s no free tier, only a 14-day trial. If you cancel, Copper deletes your data after 30 days unless you export it manually. This caught one of my teams off guard—they assumed they’d have more time to migrate.

Hidden costs pop up if you need more contacts or custom fields. Copper charges $10 per 1,000 additional contacts, and custom fields cost $5 each beyond the plan limit. For a team of 10 with 75,000 contacts, that’s an extra $250/month on the Professional plan.

## Features that actually matter

Copper’s strength is its simplicity. The interface is clean, with a focus on contacts and deals. There’s no clutter of features you’ll never use, which is refreshing compared to Salesforce or HubSpot.

The pipeline view is visual and easy to update. You can drag deals between stages, and Copper logs the change automatically. This is a step up from spreadsheets, where tracking deal movement is manual.

Email tracking is built in. Copper shows when a prospect opens an email and clicks a link. This is useful for sales teams, though it’s not as detailed as tools like Yesware or Mixmax.

The reporting is basic but functional. You can track deal velocity, win rates, and sales rep activity. There’s no advanced forecasting or custom dashboards, so if you need deep analytics, you’ll need to export data to Google Sheets or a BI tool.

## Where Copper falls short

No native Slack integration. Copper has a Zapier connection, but it’s clunky. Teams that rely on Slack for internal updates will find this frustrating. You can’t @mention a Copper record in Slack or get deal notifications without setting up a zap.

Limited customization. Copper doesn’t let you build custom objects or fields for unique workflows. If you need to track something outside contacts, companies, or deals, you’re out of luck. This is a problem for teams with complex sales processes.

No built-in phone dialer. Copper integrates with third-party tools like Aircall, but there’s no native calling. If you make a lot of cold calls, this adds friction.

The search function is weak. Copper doesn’t index email content or Drive files, so finding old conversations or documents is harder than it should be. For teams that rely on search to surface context, this is a real drawback.

## Who Copper is for

Copper works best for small teams (5-20 people) that live in Google Workspace and want a simple CRM. If you’re using Gmail, Drive, and Calendar daily, the integration saves time. It’s a good fit for agencies, consultants, and small sales teams that don’t need advanced features.

It’s not a good fit for teams that need deep customization, advanced reporting, or tight Slack integration. If you’re already using another CRM and considering a switch, the migration effort isn’t trivial. Copper’s import tool works, but you’ll need to clean up data afterward.

HubSpot CRM is the closest competitor. It has a free tier, more features, and better integrations. But it’s heavier, with a steeper learning curve. Streak is simpler and cheaper, but it’s more of a task manager than a full CRM. Salesforce Essentials is powerful but overkill for small teams.

## What to watch for

Copper was acquired by a private equity firm in 2025. Since then, updates have slowed. The roadmap is light on new features, with most releases focused on bug fixes. If you’re considering Copper, ask about the product direction. Teams that need a CRM with active development might want to look elsewhere.

The mobile app is due for an update. Copper’s team has acknowledged the limitations, but there’s no timeline for offline mode or better editing tools. If mobile access is critical, test the app thoroughly during your trial.

## The bottom line

Copper CRM is a solid choice for Google Workspace teams that want a simple, integrated CRM. The G Suite integration works, but it’s not flawless. The pricing is high for what you get, and the lack of customization will frustrate teams with unique workflows.

If you’re a small team that lives in Gmail and needs basic deal tracking, Copper is worth a try. Sign up for the 14-day trial, import a few contacts, and test the sidebar with real emails. If it saves you time, it’s a good fit. If you hit limitations quickly, look at HubSpot or Streak instead.

For teams that need more than Copper offers, the migration effort isn’t worth it. Stick with what you have or invest in a more flexible tool from the start.
